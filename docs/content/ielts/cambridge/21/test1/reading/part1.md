<div style="text-align:center;"><span style="font-size:18px;"><strong>The Davies Sisters</strong></span></div><p><br><i>Between 1908 and 1924, Gwendoline and Margaret Davies amassed one of&nbsp;the largest collections of late-nineteenth and early-twentieth-century French&nbsp;paintings in Britain</i>&nbsp;<br><br>Gwendoline (1882–1951) and Margaret (1884–1963) Davies were the granddaughters&nbsp;of David Davies, a Welshman who amassed a fortune in the shipping and mining&nbsp;industries. In 1907, when Gwendoline came into her inheritance (Margaret would follow&nbsp;in 1909), the sisters were said to be the wealthiest unmarried women in Britain. Their&nbsp;religious upbringing in rural Wales gave them a deep sense of social responsibility and&nbsp;they chose to use their inheritance for cultural and philanthropic* purposes.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br><br>While there was no real family history of art collecting, the sisters' education was&nbsp;rigorously geared toward such pursuits. Their London school focused on cultural rather&nbsp;than academic study, and they travelled extensively with their governess, Jane Blaker,&nbsp;visiting art galleries and making extensive notes on the collections there.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br><br>The sisters began to make regular art purchases from 1908, which roughly coincided&nbsp;with the dates of their inheritance. They took advice from various people, including&nbsp;the art dealer Hugh Blaker (the brother of Jane Blaker) and David Croal Thompson,&nbsp;who was also an art dealer. While it was long assumed that these men were largely responsible for the nature of the sisters' collection, it has recently been accepted that&nbsp;Gwendoline and Margaret retained a far more active role in the process.&nbsp;<br><br>The sisters' journals reveal their preference for Old Master** paintings. Yet they initially&nbsp;made very few attempts to secure any such works. While the sisters were wealthy in relative terms, their income was nothing compared to the fortunes of American art&nbsp;collectors of this period, such as J. Pierpont Morgan. Quite simply, high quality Old&nbsp;Master works were, if not beyond their means, then beyond what they were willing to&nbsp;pay for them. Instead, their early purchases were of the fashionable, safe variety, and&nbsp;included, for example, paintings by the French artist Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot.&nbsp;<br><br>However, it was only a few years before their collecting took a new direction and they&nbsp;turned to the work of the French Impressionists***. We know that Hugh Blaker, as a&nbsp; champion of contemporary French art had a hand in the decision, and we know also that&nbsp;they would have seen examples on their various trips to Paris. Whatever the precise reason&nbsp;for this change, their first purchases of Impressionist art, made in October 1912, were&nbsp;scenes of Venice by the French artist Claude Monet. Over the next 12 years, the sisters&nbsp;amassed the bulk of their Impressionist collection, including six further works by Monet,&nbsp;two more by Manet, and three by Renoir, including his well-known painting La Parisienne.&nbsp;<br><br>The First World War (1914-1918) played a part in the development of the sisters'&nbsp;collection. Their initial response to the war effort was to finance the safe passage of&nbsp;artists from occupied Belgium to Wales, as a humanitarian act, but also with the hope of&nbsp;establishing a vibrant artists' community in the area. Later in the conflict, both sisters decided to volunteer at a canteen for troops at Troyes, in northern France; Gwendoline&nbsp;in 1916, followed by Margaret in 1917.&nbsp;<br><br>It was tedious and distressing work, which would have a permanent effect on Gwendoline's&nbsp;health. Yet on one of numerous trips to nearby Paris, she visited the Bernheim-Jeune&nbsp;Gallery. Here she acquired two works by Cézanne – Provençal Landscape and The François&nbsp;Zola Dam. The paintings were shipped directly to Bath, England, where they became the&nbsp;first works by Cézanne to go on display in a public gallery in Britain.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br><br>Commentators have often described the sisters as unlikely pioneer collectors. Much is&nbsp;made of their isolation in rural Wales and the fact that they didn't make friends with artists or gallery owners. Yet they didn't feel obliged to follow fashionable tastes and&nbsp;were free to pursue their own preferences. Although they relied on a trusted circle of advisers, they made frequent trips to London and Paris, and also regularly had paintings&nbsp;sent to their home for consideration.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br><br>By the early 1920s, Gwendoline felt increasingly uncomfortable buying art works&nbsp;when faced with the poverty and social upheaval created by the First World War. Her philanthropic pursuits then became focused almost exclusively on social causes and the&nbsp;development of the sisters' home at Gregynog Hall into a conference center and venue&nbsp;for the Gregynog Festival of Music and Poetry. Gwendoline made her final art purchase&nbsp;in March 1926. Margaret also stopped collecting around this time, but started again in&nbsp;the 1930s acquiring, on a relatively small scale, work by contemporary British artists.&nbsp;<br><br>The sisters collected French Impressionist paintings at a time when such art was&nbsp;routinely ignored by individuals and institutions alike. The Gwendoline and Margaret&nbsp;Davies collection, donated in 1951 and 1963 respectively to the National Museum&nbsp;Wales, contains major examples of work by leading French Impressionists. In collecting&nbsp;paintings that they loved, the sisters created a lasting and meaningful cultural legacy for&nbsp;the people of Wales and beyond.&nbsp;&nbsp;<br><br><span style="font-size:12px;">* philanthropic: seeking to promote the welfare of others, often by charitable funding&nbsp;</span><br><span style="font-size:12px;">** Old Master: a highly respected artist of great skill who worked in Europe before about 1800</span><br><span style="font-size:12px;">*** Impressionist: an artist with a style of painting that developed in France in the late 1800s by Renoir, including his well-known painting&nbsp;La Parisienne</span></p>
